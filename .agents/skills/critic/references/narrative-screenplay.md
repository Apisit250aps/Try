# Narrative, screenplay and close reading

Use the brief and actual material; these are diagnostic lenses, not mandatory
story templates. Support: [P01–P04/P08/P16](evidence-map.md).

## Start with an argument about the work

Briefly state the intended experience and an evidenced reading: which choices
support, complicate or undermine it? Name a strength and its mechanism so a fix
does not destroy it. Summarize only enough plot to locate that reasoning.
Relate action, dialogue and formal presentation; an analysis transferable
unchanged to a prose synopsis misses the film's audiovisual realization.

Possible lenses include formal patterns, genre/history, focalization, thematic
contradictions, cultural/representational context and audience information.
Say which lens is being used. A symbolic interpretation needs repeated or
located cues and alternative explanations; a prop color alone cannot prove a
universal psychological meaning. Distinguish a character's assertion from the
work's endorsed message, and intended meaning from possible unintended impact.

## Causality and intentional ambiguity

For a suspected hole, compare three separate ledgers:

1. **World facts:** selected rules, chronology, physical/causal events.
2. **Character state:** knowledge, beliefs (possibly mistaken), motives, options.
3. **Audience disclosure:** what has actually been presented and when.

Locate the apparent contradiction on both sides. Test ellipsis, unreliable
narration, withheld explanation, genre/world rules, flashback, dream/metaphor,
episode promise and intentional ambiguity. Missing explanation is not proof of
impossibility. A “mystery” label also cannot repair two incompatible locked facts
if the supplied story excludes every exception. State scope and confidence;
when a later reveal is unavailable, call it an unresolved dependency rather than
inventing a resolution or condemning the whole work.

Distinguish an audience puzzle from dramatic irony: viewers who watched the full
answer may still fear a character's discovery. Assess against the requested
experience. Check whether the audience has enough information for the particular
choice/stakes now; fair-play clues are mandatory only if that contract is chosen.

## Theme, agency and arc

- Theme: identify a question or tension enacted by choices, contrasts and
  outcomes. A slogan repeated in dialogue is not automatically thematic depth.
  Competing readings may both be valid; ambiguity need not be “fixed.”
- Agency: map available options, pressure, actions/refusals and consequences.
  Quiet witnessing, constrained survival and deliberate inaction can matter;
  line counts or physical activity are poor substitutes for consequential agency.
- Motivation/arc: check supplied desires, values, obstacles and changes or
  deliberate steadiness. Flag a missing bridge in the given arc rather than
  require all protagonists to transform or be likable. Ensemble characters
  need differentiated functions, not equal screen time.
- Canon: separate immutable world/identity locks from episode/scene states;
  track who learns what, injuries, prop ownership and promises over revisions.
  Reuse existing fact/episode/beat IDs. Do not replace selected canon with a
  familiar genre trope or a more conventional ending.

## Scene, speech and action

Ask what this scene contributes: a decision, relation, information, rhythm,
texture, tonal counterpoint or sustained observation. Compare removing/reordering
it with losing that function; “nothing explodes” is not a scene-purpose diagnosis.
For a causal action, its necessary support must appear in the artifact; a maker's
explanation or continuity ledger cannot substitute for an absent visible event.

For dialogue/subtext, identify speaker/listener goals, risk, tactics, what is
said, what is withheld, response and change. Exposition can be accusation,
instruction, ritual, comic repetition or honest confession. Flag redundancy
with evidence and cost, not the mere existence of explicit speech. A final
direct line can be a meaningful shift after evasion. Voice depends on vocabulary,
rhythm, relationships and register; dialect is not a synonym for error.

Check action and listening, visual writing, VO/image counterpoint and who can
perceive a clue. Avoid replacing every explanation with a shot. A text review
can identify playable opportunities; performed naturalness, comic timing and
vocal authenticity need an actual read/performance and appropriate expertise.

## Emotional impact and pacing diagnosis

Describe how a setup, anticipation, recognition, reversal, reaction or release
may invite an emotion for the intended audience. “I read this as grief” is a
disclosed response, not proof all viewers will grieve. Genre/audience knowledge
and cultural context can change an inference. Propose a comprehension question
or alternate read-through instead of guaranteed engagement claims.

For “slow” or “rushed,” locate the interval/scene and expected information or
feeling. Compare: repeated information; missing goal/stakes; unclear cause;
unreadable action; too many simultaneous tasks; absent reaction; transition
misplacement; actual hold length. Name the root cause as a hypothesis unless
isolated. Trimming can remove the only readable clue; adding music can mask a
missing causal bridge. Test one targeted alternative and recheck protected beats.
