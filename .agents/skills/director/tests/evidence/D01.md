I would make **The Second Cup**: a three-minute short in which being noticed means someone remembers the small thing you need. This is a provisional original story and director's treatment, not an approved screenplay.

At closing time, a café worker stacks the chairs while the last customer, a building cleaner finishing next door, drinks tea. Each evening the worker removes the unused chair opposite the customer. Tonight the customer quietly steadies the worker's uneven stack before it falls. The worker notices the customer's tea has gone cold. Instead of clearing the table, they pour a fresh cup, return the spare chair, and sit for a moment. We end before either person finds something to say.

My position is that recognition becomes moving through a changed routine. Neither person needs a tragic backstory or a speech about loneliness. The question is: **what changes when someone stops treating another person as part of the background?** Our working answer is one interrupted task and a seat offered without ceremony.

Stay close to the worker's attention. Begin with partial views: hands wiping, chairs lifted, a coat beside a cup. The customer gradually becomes a person within that same frame, rather than arriving in an announcing close-up. Keep both visible when the customer steadies the chairs; the worker must receive that action before choosing to refill the cup. The final shared frame should have more breathing room than the opening, using rearranged furniture rather than a camera flourish.

Performance stays practical. For the customer: finish your tea without delaying closing; intervene only when the chairs need support. For the worker: get the room ready to leave, then make it possible for this person to remain a little longer. Let the returned chair carry the offer; avoid an explanatory smile held for the camera.

Use ordinary evening light, worn surfaces and a restrained warm palette. Let chair feet, wiping cloth and pouring water establish the closing rhythm. Pause that rhythm when the worker sits; no score is necessary. Allow roughly half the film for routine, a short interruption, and an unhurried final exchange of actions. Timing is an estimate.

The scale is two performers, one controllable café corner and everyday props. First rehearse the chair intervention and final seating in one shared setup. Test whether an observer can identify the moment the worker chooses to make time. If it reads as routine customer service, strengthen the stopped closing task, not the sentiment. This text is delivered; casting, location availability, rehearsal and audience effect remain untested.
