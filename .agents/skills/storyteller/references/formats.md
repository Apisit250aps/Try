# Scale, visual-first stories and serial memory

Use only the sections relevant to the medium. The following are local production
heuristics informed by [short-form craft](../research/sources.md#s23),
[serial interpretation](../research/sources.md#s24) and
[storyboard responsibilities](../research/sources.md#s25).

## Short-form storytelling

Identify the one experience the available length must deliver: a decision, a
reversal of understanding, a relationship exchange, a joke or an observation.
Limit active promises, not just word count. Enter near the meaningful change;
show enough prior normality for the contrast to register. Reuse one action/object
for several functions when that remains readable. Short does not require a twist
or “hook in three seconds”; no platform retention evidence is supplied here.

For tight duration, draft a small beat set with a time budget as an estimate.
Check that viewers could notice an object, understand its relation, observe a
decision and register the result in sequence. Allow reaction time. An outline's
total seconds are planned, not observed or validated screen time.

Example: someone habitually lays two cups; they notice the second has become a
plant pot; they water it and take their own cup outside. One domestic pattern
holds absence, continuity and a changed practice. Counterexample: a sixty-second
story introduces a war, three magic systems and six generations, then resolves
them with text cards; compression has erased the experience the story promises.

For a very short prose story, a dominant question/character/event/place thread
can guide what to keep; MICE/MACE is one diagnostic, not a mandatory closure stack.
Do not import a practitioner's rough word-count formula as a validated runtime
calculator ([S23](../research/sources.md#s23)).

## Visual-first and dialogue-free

Specify what changes on the story level, what is observable and what the audience
is invited to infer. Design actions, object relationships, repeated routines,
consequences and contextual contrasts. Keep a distinction between author-known
intention and viewer-available evidence.

| Story intention | Observable evidence proposal | Uncertainty / handoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| She begins to share control | Previously she removes a helper's work; later she offers a task, waits and uses their contribution. | The exact performance is for script/direction; test whether waiting reads as invitation. |
| Time has passed | The same plant has new leaves and the caretaker's routine changes with the season. | Artist develops appearance/state variants; do not invent exact botanical growth facts. |
| A promise is remembered | The object used in the earlier promise is handled differently when the choice returns. | Requires earlier setup; the prop alone cannot convey an unknown promise. |

Perform a text-removal check when the brief forbids dialogue, VO and readable text.
Remove explanatory captions, signs, messages and symbolic labels too. Does the
story still make the essential distinction? If not, redesign the event or be
honest about ambiguity. Visual communication is contextual, not universally
unambiguous; do not make one gesture carry an unsupported cultural meaning.

Keep narrative beats separate from shots. “The viewer discovers the clerk has
kept her own letter” is a story function. “85mm ECU, rack focus, cut on the tear”
is a camera/edit prescription and outside this role. Offer readable evidence
options for collaborators without locking their solution. A written visual beat
is not a generated panel, animatic, performance or audience comprehension test.

## Episodic, serial and hybrid

Choose the return experience:

- **Episodic:** a repeatable engine generates a fresh local question/encounter;
  identify what resets and what persists. Avoid declaring all consequences erased.
- **Serial:** changes and unresolved commitments carry across installments;
  give each installment a meaningful unit of progress, not only a withheld ending.
- **Hybrid:** close a local question while a relationship/world question advances.
  The local payoff should work even if it raises a new question.

Design an engine as **recurring situation + renewable source of variation +
constraints + consequential response**. “A crew travels” is a setting; “a repair
crew must decide what to restore when each customer's idea of repair differs”
creates repeatable narrative questions. Change the moral cost, perspective,
relationship or information pattern across episodes, not only the broken object.

Track episode entry state, local change, exit state, promises/payoffs and who
learned each fact. Separate release order from chronology for prequels/flashbacks.
A recap supplies audience memory; it does not teach a character anything.
Mittell describes how accumulated serial knowledge supports inference about
interiority ([S24](../research/sources.md#s24)); a first-time viewer lacks that
history. Decide what a new entry point must establish without duplicating the
entire bible. Do not assume every audience watches in release order if the
distribution brief says otherwise.

At season level, map direction and open obligations without freezing every future
event. Avoid cliffhanger debt that endlessly replaces promised answers. An
anthology can share a theme or world without sharing character knowledge.

## Continuity and revision at scale

Maintain one narrative source of truth using the
[production contract](production-contract.md), rather than separate character,
world and episode files that restate every fact. Split records only when volume
or ownership makes that useful. Reference Artist's appearance records by ID/version;
do not duplicate its art bible in the narrative bible.

For each revision trace **fact/rule → affected character choices → clue/setup →
episode/beat → ending → downstream script/art**. Mark updated, still valid,
pending and superseded records. A later revelation can reinterpret a fact;
changing the established event itself is a retcon and needs authorized revision
scope. Tell recipients which previously supplied packet is now stale.
